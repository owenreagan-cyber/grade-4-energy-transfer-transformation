# Answer Key — Crash Lab (Chapter 5)

- **Artifact:** `production/worksheets/05-ch5-crash-lab-student.docx`
- **Source alignment:** TG Lesson 11 (Investigating Collisions) · AP 11.1–11.2 · Reader pp. 25–30

## Big Question
What happens when objects collide? How are collisions predictable?

## Model (locked)
COLLISION → MOTION CAN CHANGE → ENERGY CAN TRANSFER → ENERGY DOES NOT DISAPPEAR

## PART A — SPEED
| Item | Correct/expected response | Acceptable alternatives | Expected pattern | Common misconception | What NOT to penalize |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Predict | Any reasonable prediction ("the target moves farthest at 3 books"). | — | stated before test | — | Not graded for correctness | wild-but-reasoned guesses |
| Test (data table) | Target displacement recorded in cm for each ramp height (1/2/3 books), 3 trials each + average. | any plausible cm values | higher ramp → greater displacement | — | "measured speed/energy directly" | slight measurement variance |
| Compare | Highest ramp (3 books) moved the target farthest. | "the tallest ramp" | higher → farther | higher ramp → faster car (inferred) | "the ramp is energy" | informal wording |
| Explain | Higher ramp → faster car → more energy of motion → greater change (target moved farther). | "faster car pushed the target farther" | speed → change | students infer speed, measure change | "force made it go" | informal phrasing |

## PART B — MASS / WEIGHT
| Item | Correct/expected response | Acceptable alternatives | Expected pattern | Common misconception | What NOT to penalize |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Predict | Any reasonable prediction ("3 cars moves the target farthest"). | — | stated before test | — | Not graded for correctness | — |
| Test (data table) | Target displacement in cm for 1/2/3 cars, 3 trials each + average. | any plausible cm values | heavier → greater displacement | — | "measured weight of energy" | measurement variance |
| Compare | Heaviest car (3 cars) moved the target farthest. | "the heaviest" | heavier → farther | same ramp → about same speed | "heavier is always faster" | informal wording |
| Explain | At about the same speed, a heavier car has more energy of motion → greater change. | "heavier car had more energy" | mass → change (same speed) | same speed + more mass → more energy | confound "heavier" with "faster" | informal phrasing |

## PUT IT TOGETHER
| Item | Correct/expected response | Acceptable alternatives | Common misconception | What NOT to penalize |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Did energy disappear? | NO — energy transferred to the target (it moved) and some became sound (+ a tiny bit of heat). | "energy moved to the cup and became sound" | "energy disappeared / was used up" | "a tiny bit of heat" optional |

## Notes
- Do NOT require momentum, impulse, force, contact force, or deformation terms.
- Sound = qualitative (loud/quiet) — no decibel measurement.
- Heat = not measured; "a tiny bit of heat" is optional source-aligned language.
- Reject any claim that energy "disappeared."

## Variable-isolation check (the key guardrail)
- Part A: only ramp height changes (same car/target/surface). Part B: only mass changes (same ramp/target/surface).
- Any response that changes BOTH speed and mass in one comparison is a confound — redirect, do not award full credit for the "fair test" reasoning.
